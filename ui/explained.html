Little Brains

Your controller, plus a cerebellum, in three lines

A tiny drop-in learning module for ordinary code. It watches the part of your system that already works, learns the errors that part keeps having to fix, and quietly starts fixing them in advance. No training phase, no GPU, no tuning — and provably silent until it has earned the right to act.

u  = controller.step(error)        // whatever you already have
u += brain.step(ctx, u)            // the entire integration
apply(u)

Part I · How it works

It bolts onto the side of a loop you already have

Almost every machine is a loop: measure, compare to what you wanted, push back against the difference. A thermostat, a PID on a robot joint, a line follower — all reflexes. Reflexes are honest but dumb: they only act after the error appears, and they make the same correction every lap, forever, without ever noticing the pattern.

The brain sits beside that loop. It never replaces your controller and it never learns your goal — it only sees what the loop can already observe (the context) and a number saying how things are going (the teacher). Its output is added to your controller's output. That's the whole surgery.

This is not that kind of neural network

When people hear "it learns," they picture deep learning: many layers, millions of weights, all of them trained by backpropagation, on a GPU, in a separate training phase, on collected data. This is a different animal — older, smaller, and better suited to living inside a running machine.

The wiring is stolen from your cerebellum

Your cortex deliberates. Your cerebellum — the "little brain" at the back of your skull, holding more than half your neurons — does something much narrower: it learns to predict and cancel the errors of your body's reflexes. It's why you can touch your nose with your eyes closed, and why the tenth sip from a new coffee mug doesn't spill like the first. This module is that circuit, transliterated:

Why copy this and not the cortex? Because the cerebellum's job description matches the engineering ask exactly: fast, cheap, always-on error prediction bolted beside an existing reflex — and it does it with a circuit simple enough that we can prove things about the copy.

How it actively learns: it watches your reflexes work

The cleverest part isn't in the brain — it's in what we feed it. The best teaching signal turns out to be your controller's own output. Think of what that number means: it is, by definition, exactly what had to be done that your automation didn't see coming. So the brain's homework, every tick, is: "given what I could observe a moment ago, predict what the reflex is having to do right now." Once it predicts well, its prediction gets added to the action — arriving before the error instead of after. The reflex finds less and less left to fix. Authorship of the behaviour transfers.

This also explains what it can and can't do. It cancels everything predictable: gravity sag, a bent wheel, a periodic vibration, a payload's weight, a track's curvature. It cannot cancel true surprises — and it doesn't try.

How it knows it's helping: confidence is earned, not assumed

A learner that acts while ignorant makes things worse — we measured it, and it's ugly. So the output passes through a competence gate. The brain studies silently at first: full learning, zero output. It watches the correlation between what it would have said and what the reflex actually did. Only when its predictions demonstrably match reality does the gate open, gradually. Two memories, like yours

One memory can't both adapt in seconds and remember for good — chase the present and you forget the past. So there are two, learning the same lesson at different speeds, exactly as motor memory does (it's why a skill survives a night's sleep even after a bad day).

The honest boundary

The brain can only learn what its context can see.

We built a maze demo to find the edge, and found it. The brain measurably learned what a dead-end mouth looks like from raw wall patterns — real learned structure — yet barely gained, because a maze's wasted steps come from deep branches that no local glance can predict. No amount of cleverness conjures information that isn't in the inputs. If your problem's answer isn't a function of what you can observe, this module will honestly learn nothing, its gate will stay shut, and your system will run exactly as before. That failure mode — quiet, harmless, visible — is a feature we paid for.

Part II · The main page

What this is

A goal-blind, constant-memory, gradient-free learning sidecar for loops and decision points. You keep your algorithm. The brain attaches beside it, watches its corrections, and takes over the predictable ones — so your simple controller performs like a much smarter one, without you deriving anything.

It is

  • three lines in your existing loop
  • learning that happens while running
  • self-tuning — constants derive from loop rate + authority
  • tiny: microcontroller-class CPU and RAM
  • guarded: never worse than what you had
  • inspectable: watch its cells fire, watch skill consolidate

It is not

  • a replacement for control theory when you need certificates
  • an RL agent that invents strategies
  • a fix for information your sensors don't carry
  • an LLM, or anything that needs one
  • a reason to skip making your baseline basically work

When to reach for it

The tell is a sentence you've probably said about your own project:

  • "It works, but it always sags/overshoots in the same spot."
  • "Every lap, same wobble. Every layer, same ripple."
  • "It was tuned for no payload — now everything's off."
  • "I'd need the full dynamics model to fix this properly, and I'm not doing that."
  • "It works on my bench but drifts in the field."

Repeatable error + observable context = food. Robot arms, line followers, drones and planes holding a path, 3D printers, camera gimbals, thermostats and kilns, ship and antenna pointing, ballistics and throwing machines, scoring functions inside solvers — anything with a loop and a pattern.

Proven, live, in your browser

The three decisions you actually make

Not tuning — engineering, once, in plain language:

  • Teacher: feed it your controller's own output — "learn what my reflex keeps having to do." (For episodic tasks: the miss, converted to output units, as a residual on top of what the brain already said.)
  • Context: give it what the correction depends on. Position-locked effects need position channels; if the brain plateaus, the cure is a missing observation, not a knob.
  • Authority: declare your controller's maximum output. The brain confines itself to half of that, forever.

Where this is going

Step one of a larger idea: ambient adaptation — a little mathematical reasoning folded into ordinary code, no LLM anywhere. Today your program's competence is frozen at the moment you stopped tuning it. The bet here is that most software that touches the physical world deserves what your body already has: a small, cheap, trustworthy layer that notices "I keep making this same correction" and quietly absorbs it — so a robot arm ripens with use, an A* solver's cost function sharpens to its terrain, a printer learns its own resonances, and none of it requires the author to become a controls engineer or bolt on a model server.

The dream shape is boring on purpose: a commodity block, the way PID itself became one. #include "brain.h", one object, one added line, and a serial-printable map of cells so you can literally watch it think. Algorithms stop being ceilings and start being floors — everything you ship gets a little better every hour it runs, and provably never worse.

Little Brains · linear readout over a frozen random basis · LMS (1960) + CMAC (Albus 1975) + feedback-error learning (Kawato 1990) + two-rate memory (Smith 2006), packaged with a competence gate and a testbench. The contribution is the packaging — and the honesty.
